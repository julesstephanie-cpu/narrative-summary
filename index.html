Why this is urgent

We can win this November and still be standing at the starting line in 2030

Every two years, Democratic operatives build campaigns for candidates and then take them apart. Staff moves on. Data goes stale. Relationships are built, then gone before the next campaign starts.

The other side spent fifty years building institutions that were never meant to come down. You can see the results in the courts, in school boards, and in state houses. Five organizations — founded across six decades, every one still operating — form a compounding machine. No single donation built it. No single election broke it.

What the name means

Our name claims the eighty percent of Americans who agree on the majority of policy and want the same handful of things. That common ground runs through most of the Democratic Party. It lives in the center-left, and it includes independents, moderates, and a great many people who tuned out because nothing on offer sounded like them.

02The GapExecutive Summary · 2 of 7

Three structural problems prevent the model from replicating itself

PROBLEM 01 No national vehicle exists to fund it

Individual state parties can build this infrastructure, as Michigan proved. Nothing systematically capitalizes the build in other states.

PROBLEM 02 No standing entity translates the lessons

The specialist-to-county ratio, the training curriculum, the recruitment pipeline — all of it lives in Michigan's institutional memory. None of it has been codified into a transferable model.

PROBLEM 03 No permanent talent or brand layer above the state

No national organization's full-time job is identifying which state parties are underperforming, why, and what a rebuild costs — the way Heritage and its peers do continuously for the right.

03Proof of ConceptExecutive Summary · 3 of 7
Evidence

Our model is not theoretical. It has been built successfully twice at the state level.

Michigan proves it at the field-organizing level. Colorado proves it at the capital-and-institution level, and has now run for more than two decades.

Proof point one — Project 83, Michigan

In 2017, Michigan Democratic Party Chair Lavora Barnes launched Project 83 — named for Michigan's 83 counties — on three tenets: refocus on local parties and local candidates, retrain local leadership and grassroots activists, rebuild the Democratic brand in communities the party had ceded. Not a cycle-specific field program: a standing network of regional Political Specialists working year-round.

Why The 2024 Michigan Loss is Important

Infrastructure that survives a bad cycle instead of evaporating with it is the entire argument for treating this as an investment rather than a campaign expense. Project 83 is proof the model works. It is also proof the model needs patient, multi-cycle capital to reach its potential — Michigan built it inside one state party's existing budget. The 80's job is to do deliberately, with dedicated capital and a national blueprint, what Michigan discovered by necessity.

Proof point two — the Colorado Blueprint

Colorado was, as recently as 2002, one of the most reliably Republican states in the country. Beginning in 2004, a small group of donors — Pat Stryker, Tim Gill, Jared Polis, and Rutt Bridges, organized with the help of retired university president Albert Yates — built a coordinated funding vehicle that became the Colorado Democracy Alliance. The strategy and the reversal it produced are chronicled in the 2010 book The Blueprint: How the Democrats Won Colorado, co-authored by a Democratic journalist and a former Republican state legislator.

What Colorado Actually Built

A funding collaborative

Members-only, directing pooled donor capital to more than 30 aligned nonprofits and independent-expenditure groups — instead of one donor writing one check to one campaign.

One disciplined message

A consistent statewide message carried across every funded organization, rather than each group improvising its own.

Locally-rooted candidates

Contenders with genuine ties to their communities, not outside recruits parachuted into targeted districts.

Big-tent discipline

A shared willingness to set aside internal policy disagreements and unify around winning — The 80's own "Rebuild the Big Tent" principle.

The two proofs together

Project 83 shows what disciplined, permanent field infrastructure looks like inside a single state party. Colorado shows what happens when that infrastructure is capitalized deliberately, across a full ecosystem of organizations, and sustained for decades. The 80 is built to combine both: field infrastructure funded the way Colorado's donor coalition funded its own.

04What We Are BuildingExecutive Summary · 4 of 7
The solution

One 501(c)(4) with three integrated functions

Permanent capacity, assembled from the contested states outward.

FUNCTION 01 Capital Investment & Operational Support

Funds and stands up the organizer and regional-lead field structure. Partners with, invests in, or acquires existing state infrastructure rather than duplicating it.

FUNCTION 02 Agency: Media & Technology

Shared research, message testing, and creative capacity, so every state build carries one brand and real-time data instead of reinventing a messaging shop from zero.

FUNCTION 03 Civic & Political

Recruits, trains, and supports the candidates the infrastructure exists to elect. Rebuilds local parties and incubates activist networks.

Why a 501(c)(4)

The entity structure follows directly from the problem being solved.

  1. Multi-year commitments. A (c)(4) can hold and deploy capital on a five-year horizon rather than being bound to election-cycle reporting and spending patterns — which is what permanent infrastructure requires and what campaign committees structurally cannot do.
  2. Partisan flexibility within a social-welfare mission. A (c)(4) can engage in political activity as part of — not the entirety of — its mission, allowing The 80 to fund party infrastructure, civic organizations, and issue advocacy under one roof rather than fragmenting the work across single-purpose vehicles.
  3. Donor structure suited to patient capital. Contributions are not tax-deductible as charitable gifts. That is the tradeoff for the flexibility above, and it is stated plainly in every donor conversation and in the closing disclosure of every 80 presentation.
  4. A holding structure for a portfolio, not a single program. The 80 is meant to partner with, invest in, and in some cases acquire existing organizations — state parties, civic orgs, policy shops — rather than build every function from scratch. A (c)(4) is structured for exactly that.

Selection & sequencing

Not every state should be built the same way or in the same order

What persuades a voter in Michigan will not persuade one in Georgia. Four criteria we will use to choose how and when to invest in a state.

CriterionGuiding question
Electoral leverageDoes building durable infrastructure here plausibly change control of a legislative chamber, a statewide office, or a congressional delegation within two cycles?
Existing infrastructure conditionIs the state party a foundation to invest in and strengthen — a "performance turnaround," as Michigan's own party once was — or a near-total rebuild from the ground up?
Cost to stand up coverageWhat does a beachhead-to-full-coverage build cost, driven primarily by county count and the organizer-to-county ratio validated in Michigan?
Proof-of-concept valueWill an early, visible win here make the next state easier to fund — i.e., does it demonstrate the model to future donors the way Michigan now demonstrates it to us?
What comes next

Individual state models — organizer counts, regional-lead management layers, phased budgets, and timelines — are built one state at a time against this framework, starting with the pilot track.

06The Operating ModelExecutive Summary · 6 of 7
How a state gets built

Rome Wasn't Built in a Day

Michigan did not staff all 83 counties on day one. Project 83 grew into full coverage over multiple cycles. The 80 replicates that discipline deliberately rather than rediscovering it under pressure.

What phasing does that a lump-sum build cannot

It gives donors a visible, fundable proof point before committing to full-state cost — and it mirrors exactly how Michigan's own model actually developed, which is the strongest evidence available that it works.

Foundational operating tenets — from day one, in every state

  • Use local talent wherever possible; create a mentor/coach role while a young organizer comes up to speed.
  • Have every organizer become a notary.
  • Monthly, in-person, statewide organizer meetings — moved around the state, held midweek, each including a novel skill class such as first aid or self-defense.
  • A weekly call where organizers share successes and challenges and get input from the group.
  • Encourage cooperative programming between regions.
  • Debrief volunteers and staff from the top levels down — it makes people feel valued and drives participation.
  • Humanize the organization. Our people should be the face of the movement.
  • Keep diversity a top priority at every level.

07The AskExecutive Summary · 7 of 7
Capital position

$30 million to carry us through launch and into 2027 at full speed

Anyone asked to invest in infrastructure is owed a look at the infrastructure — and an understanding of the return.

WHY US The model has been run twice, successfully

Project 83 was built by a member of this team. The Colorado Democracy Alliance has been compounding for twenty-two years. The 80 combines both: field infrastructure funded the way Colorado's donor coalition funded its own.

WHY NOW 2028 and beyond will be decided by what gets built now

This raise carries The 80 through launch and into 2027 at full speed. Infrastructure built on a cycle timeline arrives too late to matter; the beachhead has to exist before the cycle it is meant to win.

WHY YOU Patient capital is the one input the model cannot improvise

Michigan proved the model works inside an ordinary state-party budget. It also proved the model needs multi-cycle capital to reach its potential. That is the gap a donor closes here.

ROI Targets

The 80 targets a return in excess of any 10× benchmark, measured against the cost of rebuilding equivalent capability from scratch each cycle. The underlying model, assumptions, and reporting cadence sit in the Budget, ROI & Reporting chapter.

By the last page, you will know what we intend to build in each state, who is accountable for it, and what it costs. David Pachter · CEO

What this framework unlocks next

With this scaffolding in place, the next step is building individual, fully costed state models — organizer counts, regional-lead management layers, phased budgets, and timelines — for each state on the priority track, starting with the pilot. Each state model will:

  • Apply the organizer-to-county ratio and phased beachhead structure defined here.
  • Layer in state-specific adjustments — population density, competitiveness of target counties, condition of existing party infrastructure — rather than a flat per-county formula.
  • Report against the same three-function framework, so donors can compare state opportunities on a consistent basis.
What this document is, and is not

This is not the five-year plan. It is the case for why a five-year plan, funded like an investment portfolio rather than an election-cycle expense, is the only version of this that has ever actually worked.
